# What Makes Apps Pop Off — Research Notes for Gazelle.io

Status date: 2026-06-23
Purpose: research-mode synthesis for Laith on what makes new apps break through, then translate it into a launch/product strategy for Gazelle.io.

## Executive answer

Apps pop off when they combine five things:

1. A painfully clear wedge
   - People instantly understand what problem it solves or why it is provocative.
   - Examples: Cluely = “cheat on everything”; Cal AI = photo of food to calories; NotebookLM = docs to AI podcast; Lovable/Bolt = prompt to working app.

2. A 3-second demo
   - The product must be explainable visually before anyone reads copy.
   - The best demos show input → transformation → output.

3. A shareable artifact
   - The user creates something worth sharing: a video, app, note, podcast, score, report, progress snapshot, or “look what I built/learned” proof.

4. Founder-led distribution
   - The founder becomes the narrator, protagonist, and media channel.
   - Boring company pages rarely carry launches. Personal posts, demos, and founder story do.

5. A distribution loop that matches the product
   - Consumer apps: TikTok/Reels/Shorts, controversy, identity, remixing.
   - Prosumer apps: LinkedIn/X, workflow proof, shared docs/artifacts.
   - Edtech: teachers, parents, classrooms, progress reports, trust, community referrals.

For Gazelle, the best path is not Cluely-style controversy. Gazelle deals with kids, teachers, parents, and trust. The winning strategy is:

> Make Gazelle feel like the safest, clearest demo of “AI learning with adult oversight,” then create a shareable artifact: a parent-friendly learning snapshot or teacher-ready lesson report.

Do not launch as “AI tutor for kids.” That is generic and trust-dangerous.
Launch as:

> “I built a K-6 learning demo where AI is useful only after teacher/parent guardrails: lesson plans are reviewable, tutor answers are filtered, and parents get plain-English progress summaries.”

## The big pattern from new viral AI apps

### Cluely

Sources:
- TechCrunch: https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/20/cluely-a-startup-that-helps-cheat-on-everything-raises-15m-from-a16z/
- SF Standard: https://sfstandard.com/2025/07/18/cluely-startups-roy-lee-columbia-cheating-viral-tiktok/
- NBC on Interview Coder / Columbia controversy: https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/columbia-university-student-trolls-big-tech-ai-tool-job-applications-rcna198454

What made it pop:
- Clear, morally explosive wedge: “cheat on everything.”
- Founder became the character: Columbia suspension, dropout, controversial CEO.
- Demo-first social content: hidden AI assistant in dating/interviews/calls.
- Engineered controversy: people shared it because they hated/loved/debated it.
- Creator distribution: SF Standard reported creator/clipping operations around the company.

Lesson for Gazelle:
- Cluely proves that boring AI positioning loses.
- But Gazelle cannot copy the ethics. Kids + education require trust, not chaos.
- What Gazelle can copy: compressed message, founder-led demos, clear social debate.

A safer Gazelle controversy angle:
- “AI tutors for kids are dangerous unless adults stay in the loop.”
- “I built the anti-black-box AI tutor: every output is logged, filtered, and reviewable.”
- “The hard part is not calling an LLM. It is designing the guardrails around kids.”

### Lovable / Bolt

Sources:
- Lovable blog: https://lovable.dev/blog/2025-01-29-zero-to-10m-arr-in-2-months
- Madrona on StackBlitz/Bolt: https://www.madrona.com/investing-in-stackblitz
- a16z consumer AI 2025: https://www.a16z.news/p/state-of-consumer-ai-2025

What made them pop:
- Immediate magic: prompt → working app.
- User output is shareable: “look what I built.”
- Strong developer/builder audience on X/LinkedIn/YouTube.
- Clear before/after: before you need coding; after you have a running prototype.

Lesson for Gazelle:
- Gazelle needs its own shareable output.
- Best candidates:
  1. Teacher lesson snapshot: objective → generated lesson → question bank → approval gate.
  2. Parent learning snapshot: “Your child practiced fractions, got stuck on X, next practice is Y.”
  3. Student confidence moment: “I got unstuck” screenshot or mini-progress card.

### NotebookLM

Sources:
- a16z consumer AI 2025: https://www.a16z.news/p/state-of-consumer-ai-2025
- ZDNET NotebookLM review: https://www.zdnet.com/article/i-used-notebooklm-for-an-entire-month-heres-why-it-really-is-a-game-changer/

What made it pop:
- The underlying product was source-grounded notes/Q&A, but the breakout feature was Audio Overviews.
- Output became the viral artifact: “listen to this AI podcast about my docs.”

Lesson for Gazelle:
- The killer demo may not be the whole app. It may be one artifact.
- For Gazelle, that artifact should probably be:
  - “plain-English parent summary” or
  - “teacher-ready weekly intervention report.”

### Granola

Sources:
- TechCrunch: https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/14/ai-note-taking-app-granola-raises-43m-at-250m-valuation-launches-collaborative-features/

What made it pop:
- It solved a high-frequency workflow without feeling intrusive.
- Shared notes created a private workplace virality loop.

Lesson for Gazelle:
- For teachers, the product must feel like workflow relief, not “another AI tool.”
- The teacher loop should be:
  - create lesson faster
  - approve it
  - see student evidence
  - send parent-readable summary

### Sora / Friend / Cal AI

Sources:
- Sora TechCrunch: https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/30/openai-is-launching-the-sora-app-its-own-tiktok-competitor-alongside-the-sora-2-model/
- Friend coverage: https://observer.com/2025/10/avi-schiffmann-ceo-ai-startup-friend-backlash/
- Cal AI TikTok/influencer summary: https://blog.funnelfox.com/cal-ai-influencer-marketing/

What made them pop:
- Sora: visual wow, invite scarcity, remix/social feed, identity/likeness.
- Friend: physical object + emotional/controversial positioning.
- Cal AI: camera input removes a daily chore; demo is obvious in seconds.

Lesson for Gazelle:
- Gazelle’s launch demo should minimize explanation.
- Show a teacher entering one objective and getting a reviewable plan + parent/student path.
- Show a parent seeing a summary that makes them say: “Oh, I understand how my kid is doing.”

## Edtech-specific patterns

### Products researched

- Duolingo: https://investors.duolingo.com/investor-relations
- Khan Academy / Khanmigo: https://www.khanacademy.org/about and https://www.khanmigo.ai/
- Quizlet: https://quizlet.com/gb/teachers and https://quizlet.com/features/ai-study-tools
- Photomath: https://photomath.com/
- ClassDojo: https://www.classdojo.com/
- MagicSchool: https://www.magicschool.ai/magic-tools and https://www.magicschool.ai/privacy
- IXL Analytics: https://www.ixl.com/analytics
- Prodigy: https://www.prodigygame.com/main-en
- Brilliant: https://www.brilliant.org/ / related review sources

### Pattern 1: Edtech must be role-specific

Teachers care about:
- saving prep time
- knowing who needs help
- standards/skills alignment
- small-group intervention
- classroom workflow
- parent communication

Parents care about:
- safety
- confidence
- “is my child actually learning?”
- “what should I do next?”
- no need to be a subject expert

Students care about:
- getting unstuck
- encouragement
- short loops
- fun/progress
- not feeling stupid

Gazelle should not have one generic landing message. It should have three role-specific promises.

### Pattern 2: Trust and safety are not legal footnotes

MagicSchool, Khan Academy, ClassDojo, Quizlet, and Prodigy all surface trust/privacy/safety in product or marketing.

For Gazelle, the safety story is a differentiator:
- student has no direct auth account
- parent owns student data
- Tutor does not expose correct answers up front
- agent outputs are logged/reviewed/filtered
- no raw student chat logs as a design goal
- service role key never touches client

This should be part of the demo, not hidden in docs.

### Pattern 3: Proof of learning matters

IXL, Khan Academy, Quizlet, and Prodigy all make progress visible.

Gazelle should emphasize:
- skill practiced
- misconception detected
- next recommended practice
- confidence/progress trend
- parent-friendly summary
- teacher intervention cue

### Pattern 4: Student delight creates pull

Duolingo, Prodigy, ClassDojo, Brilliant, Photomath all create student delight through:
- streaks
- games
- avatars
- instant feedback
- “I got it” moments
- friendly design

Gazelle does not need full gamification for launch, but the student screen must feel warm and confidence-building.

## Launch channel strategy for Gazelle

### Best primary channel: LinkedIn

Why:
- Your goal is also recruiting/portfolio signal.
- LinkedIn rewards personal builder stories and technical demos.
- Recruiters, engineers, classmates, and startup people can understand this.

Winning LinkedIn angle:

> “I built a full-stack K-6 AI learning demo, but the real work was not the AI call. It was designing adult-in-the-loop guardrails around kids: parent-owned data, teacher approval, RAG-grounded lessons, filtered tutor outputs, and plain-English progress summaries.”

Do not lead with:
- “Excited to announce”
- “revolutionizing education”
- “AI-powered adaptive learning platform” by itself

Lead with:
- problem
- demo
- what you built
- why the guardrails matter
- link/screenshots/video

### Secondary channel: GitHub / portfolio

Make the repo the proof of engineering judgment:
- architecture diagram
- verification commands
- screenshots
- demo script
- safety constraints
- tradeoffs
- what is not production-ready yet

### Optional: X/Twitter

Use for builder thread:
- “I rebuilt my portfolio as a real full-stack AI product demo.”
- Include short video/GIF.
- Technical breakdown thread.

### Not first: Product Hunt / Hacker News

Product Hunt can work after there is a live demo, screenshots, and a polished video.
Hacker News is risky unless framed technically:

> Show HN: I built a guarded AI learning demo with Supabase RLS, pgvector RAG, and reviewable tutor outputs

HN will dislike generic marketing, child AI overclaiming, and signup walls.

### TikTok/Reels/Shorts

Only worth it if you make a visual demo:
- teacher enters objective → lesson appears
- parent sees child progress card
- student gets friendly tutor help

Short-form hook examples:
- “Most AI tutors for kids skip the adult-in-the-loop problem. I built one that does not.”
- “I built a K-6 AI tutor demo where the AI is not allowed to just talk freely.”
- “The hardest part of building AI for kids is not the model. It is the guardrails.”

## What Gazelle needs to pop more

### 1. A sharper wedge

Current product is broad: teacher dashboard + parent/student iOS + agents + RAG.
That is impressive technically, but broad products are hard to spread.

Recommended wedge:

> AI lesson planning + student practice with parent/teacher guardrails.

Even sharper:

> The parent-readable learning snapshot for AI tutoring.

Why this wedge:
- Clarity for parents.
- Differentiates from generic AI tutors.
- Creates a shareable artifact.
- Makes safety visible.

### 2. One killer demo artifact

Build or fake-with-demo-data a polished “Learning Snapshot” card:

Fields:
- Student first name or demo learner
- Grade / topic
- Practiced skill
- What went well
- Where they got stuck
- Next 10-minute practice
- Parent encouragement line
- Teacher intervention note
- “Generated after filtered tutor session” badge

This is the thing to screenshot/share.

### 3. A tighter first-run demo

The walkthrough should be:

1. Teacher enters: “Grade 3 fractions as parts of a whole.”
2. Gazelle creates draft lesson.
3. Teacher approves question bank.
4. Student answers 2 questions.
5. Parent sees plain-English summary.
6. Teacher sees misconception/intervention note.

This is the full loop. Anything else is secondary.

### 4. More human copy

Less:
- “Mastra agents query pgvector RAG”

More:
- “Teachers stay in control.”
- “Parents can understand progress without being math experts.”
- “Students get short, encouraging practice.”

Keep architecture in the technical proof section.

### 5. Founder-led story

Your story should be:

> I wanted to build something more serious than an AI wrapper. So I picked a hard domain — kids learning — and focused on the boundaries: privacy, parent ownership, teacher approval, filtered outputs, and evidence of progress.

This is better for recruiters than pretending it has users.

## Proposed Gazelle launch narrative

### One-liner

Gazelle.io is a full-stack demo of AI-assisted K-6 learning where teachers stay in control, parents understand progress, and student-facing tutor output is guarded.

### Short launch pitch

I built Gazelle.io to explore a harder version of AI education: not just “chat with a tutor,” but a system with adult oversight.

Teachers can create reviewable lesson drafts, students practice through filtered tutor flows, and parents get plain-English learning snapshots. Under the hood it uses Next.js, SwiftUI, Supabase/RLS, pgvector RAG, and Mastra agent workflows.

It is a portfolio/demo build, not a production classroom product — but it shows the architecture, safety constraints, and product judgment I wanted to practice.

### Strongest LinkedIn hook

The hardest part of building an AI tutor for kids is not calling the model.

It is deciding what the model is not allowed to do.

### Technical credibility bullets

- Next.js teacher dashboard with Supabase auth and protected routes
- SwiftUI parent/student app surface
- Supabase Postgres/RLS/storage with parent-owned student boundaries
- pgvector + Voyage embeddings for curriculum RAG
- Mastra Tutor, Planner, and Curriculum agent workflows
- agent output review/filter protocol before surfacing content
- no service-role key in client surfaces
- tutor questions hide correct answers up front

## Specific next product changes I recommend

Priority 1: Create a Learning Snapshot component/page
- Add a public demo section or route showing a parent-friendly summary.
- This becomes the shareable artifact.

Priority 2: Add a 60-second demo video script and record it
- Already started in docs, but the video should follow the full loop.
- Use voiceover or captions.

Priority 3: Add three role pages/sections
- For Teachers
- For Parents
- For Students

Priority 4: Add launch analytics after approval
- PostHog or simple Vercel analytics.
- Track: landing view, CTA click, auth start, lesson generation, lesson approval.
- Do not track student/child data in third-party analytics.

Priority 5: Add waitlist/contact form only if safe
- “Interested in seeing the demo / giving feedback?”
- Avoid implying real school deployment.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Do not copy Cluely’s ethics/controversy. Copy the clarity.
- Do not claim production use, school adoption, compliance certification, or learning outcomes.
- Do not bury the product behind architecture.
- Do not launch without one shareable artifact.
- Do not make the student AI feel unbounded.
- Do not over-gamify before the trust story works.
- Do not post only a GitHub link.
